# Eating Well, Breastfeeding, Weaning, and Supplementing

_Source pages: 41-48_

_Handbook: Toddler’s First Steps, 4th edition, updated in 2024._

## Eating Well

Eating well is key to a toddler’s healthy growth and development. Children who eat well from an early age have the best chance of being healthy eaters for life. Encourage healthy eating by:

- Breastfeeding or chestfeeding, or giving expressed milk, for 2 years or longer.
- Introducing solid foods at about 6 months.
- Working toward 3 meals and 2 or 3 sit-down snacks at regular times each day.
- Providing a variety of nutritious foods and watching for cues that your toddler is hungry or full.
- Creating a calm, no-pressure eating environment and eating together as a family.
- Offering attention, affection and playtime—not food or drink—as comfort.
- Modelling healthy eating habits and ensuring other caregivers know your feeding routines.

### Mealtime Roles

You and your toddler each have a role in healthy eating. Your role is to offer a variety of healthy foods and meals and snacks at regular times each day, and respond to your toddler’s hunger and fullness cues. Your toddler’s role is to choose whether to eat, what to eat from what is offered, and how much to eat.

By doing your job with feeding and trusting your toddler to do their job with eating, you can help them become a healthy eater and have less mealtime conflict. A toddler’s appetite varies from day to day. Letting them decide how much to eat teaches them to listen to their body. If concerned about eating or growth, call HealthLink BC at 8-1-1 and ask for a registered dietitian.

### Hunger and Fullness Cues

**Hunger and fullness cues** are signs a young toddler gives when they know they are hungry or full. When food is offered, a hungry toddler may open their mouth or lean forward excitedly, kick their feet or wave their hands. A full toddler may push food away, close their mouth or turn their head away.

If your older toddler will not eat, let them leave the table. They may not be hungry, or may be tired, excited or sick. Wait until the next regular meal or snack to offer more. Do not use desserts or other treats as bribes for finishing food. If a toddler is often not hungry at mealtimes, they may be filling up on drinks. More than 3 cups (750 ml) of cow’s milk per day leaves little room for food.

Sitting down to eat together builds language and social skills and encourages healthy eating habits.

## Breastfeeding or Chestfeeding Your Toddler

Human milk remains an important part of a growing toddler’s diet after solid foods start. Breastfeeding or chestfeeding offers comfort and builds attachment, helping a toddler grow into a more confident child.

### Benefits for Your Toddler and You

Human milk:

- Is a natural and nutritious food for your toddler.
- Is all the food your child needs for the first 6 months, and continues to be important for 2 years or more.
- Supports your toddler’s immune system.
- Protects your toddler from some infections.
- Supports your toddler’s healthy growth.

Breastfeeding or chestfeeding:

- May lower your risk of diseases like breast and ovarian cancers.
- Is convenient.
- Helps you bond with your toddler.

Toddlers who breastfeed or chestfeed do not need cow’s milk or other animal milk. Until at least 1 year old, they need a liquid vitamin D supplement of 400 IU every day. Ask a health care provider or call HealthLink BC at 8-1-1 with questions.

### Common Situations

- **Returning to work or school:** Breastfeed or chestfeed before leaving and when returning home. Your toddler may want to feed more in the evening or at night to make up for time apart. You can express milk at work or school and have a caregiver give it the next day. Plan when and where to pump and where to clean the pump and store milk. B.C. employers must, by law, make this possible.
- **Taking prescribed medication:** Most medications are safe while breastfeeding or chestfeeding, but check with your health care provider, public health nurse or pharmacist, or call HealthLink BC at 8-1-1.
- **Feeding in public:** Canadian laws protect your right to breastfeed or chestfeed anywhere, at any time. It is normal, healthy and legal, and it is illegal for anyone to ask you to stop or cover up.
- **Pregnancy:** Talk with your health care provider to ensure you are getting enough nutrition for your unborn child, breastfeeding or chestfeeding toddler and yourself. Take a prenatal supplement. Around the fourth or fifth month, milk may look different and supply may decrease due to normal hormonal changes of pregnancy. Some toddlers naturally wean then, but may want to breastfeed or chestfeed again once the baby is born and more milk is flowing. If pregnant, try lying down while feeding to get extra rest.
- **Feeding a new baby too:** You can “tandem nurse”—breastfeed or chestfeed a new baby as well as your toddler. Your body adjusts to make enough milk for both. This can help you connect with both children. Feed them together or separately. In the first few days after birth, feed the newborn first so they get lots of practice and all the nutrition and antibodies in colostrum.
- **Biting or teething:** Remove the toddler from your breast or chest and firmly say biting hurts, then try again. Repeat if needed. Biting may mean they are teething (see Looking After Your Toddler’s Teeth), sick or finished feeding. A properly latched, sucking child cannot bite. Remove them as soon as they are done; watch for long pauses between sucking and break the latch by putting your index finger into their mouth.

### Tobacco, Alcohol, Cannabis, and Other Drugs

**Smoking or vaping tobacco:** It is best for you and your child to stop smoking. Breastfeeding or chestfeeding remains important for the child’s health, and many nursing parents who smoke do so successfully. It may help protect the baby from some negative effects of tobacco-smoke exposure. Smoking exposes the child to second-hand smoke and nicotine and other harmful substances that pass through milk. It can reduce milk supply and lead to earlier weaning. Quitting or cutting down is healthy for both. If you cannot quit, reduce exposure by breastfeeding or chestfeeding before smoking, keeping the car and home smoke-free, washing hands and changing clothes after smoking.

For help quitting or reducing smoking:

- Ask a pharmacist or doctor about the B.C. Smoking Cessation Program, which provides free and low-cost nicotine-replacement products and medications.
- Contact Quit Now at quitnow.ca or 1-877-455-2233 (toll-free).
- Talk Tobacco provides Indigenous quit-smoking and vaping support at smokershelpline.ca/talktobacco/home or 1-833-998-8255 (TALK).
- First Nations Health Authority resources: “Quitting Commercial Tobacco” at fnha.ca/Documents/FNHA-Quitting-Commercial-Tobacco-FAQs.pdf and “Respecting Tobacco” at fnha.ca/respectingtobacco.

**Alcohol:** The safest choice is not to drink while breastfeeding, chestfeeding or expressing milk. It takes about 2 hours for alcohol from 1 standard drink to leave the body and human milk. Having one standard drink once in a while may be okay if planned. To lower risks, stick to one standard drink; breastfeed or chestfeed (or pump and store milk) before drinking; wait 2 to 3 hours per drink before breastfeeding, chestfeeding or expressing milk again; and throw away milk expressed within 2 to 3 hours of drinking. See Canada’s Guidance on Alcohol and Health for more information. The body takes time to clear alcohol from milk; pumping, drinking lots of water, resting or coffee do not make this happen faster. Milk pumped while drinking or in the 2 to 3 hours after drinking should be discarded.

**Cannabis:** The safest option is not to use cannabis while breastfeeding or chestfeeding. THC is stored in human milk and can pass to the child. Because THC remains in the body for a long time, pumping and discarding milk does not make it safe. CBD has not been proven safe either. Cannabis can cause drowsiness and make it hard to respond to a toddler’s needs. Whether smoked, vaped, applied to skin or ingested as edibles, drinks, oils or pills, no amount is known to be safe while breastfeeding or chestfeeding. Breastfeeding or chestfeeding has many health benefits for both toddler and parent; if stopping cannabis is difficult, discuss steps to protect you and your toddler with your health care provider.

If you have more than 1 standard alcoholic drink per day, smoke, use cannabis or take non-prescription opioids and stimulants, talk with a trusted health care provider, public health nurse or pharmacist, or call HealthLink BC at 8-1-1. They can help you make the best feeding decision. To protect your child, avoid exposing them to smoke from cannabis, vaping, e-cigarettes and cigarettes during pregnancy and after birth.

**Non-prescription opioids and stimulants:** These can pass through milk and affect your toddler. If you use them and want support and information about safe breastfeeding or chestfeeding, talk with a trusted health care provider. Keep all alcohol, cannabis, tobacco, medications and non-prescription opioids and stimulants out of your toddler’s reach.

### Canada’s Guidance on Alcohol and Health

Canada’s guidance supports informed decisions about alcohol and encourages people to consider reducing their alcohol use. A standard drink means:

- Beer: 341 ml (12 oz) of beer at 5% alcohol.
- Cooler or cider, ready-to-drink: 341 ml (12 oz) at 5% alcohol.
- Wine: 142 ml (5 oz) at 12% alcohol.
- Spirits (whisky, vodka, gin, etc.): 43 ml (1.5 oz) at 40% alcohol.

The source chart describes weekly alcohol consumption this way:

- **0 drinks per week — no risk:** Not drinking has benefits, such as better health and better sleep.
- **1 to 2 standard drinks per week — low risk:** You will likely avoid alcohol-related consequences for yourself and others.
- **3 to 6 standard drinks per week — moderate risk:** Your risk of developing several different types of cancer, including breast and colon cancer, increases.
- **7 or more standard drinks per week — increasingly high risk:** Your risk of heart disease or stroke increases. Each additional standard drink radically increases the risk of these alcohol-related consequences.

If you are going to drink, do not exceed 2 drinks on any day. Drinking less benefits you and others. It reduces your risk of injury and violence, and many health problems that can shorten life. During pregnancy or when trying to get pregnant, there is no known safe amount of alcohol use. When breastfeeding, not drinking alcohol is the safest. Health risks increase more quickly at 7 or more standard drinks per week for females. Overall, far more injuries, violence and deaths result from men’s drinking.

The Canadian Centre on Substance Use and Addiction was commissioned by Health Canada to produce Canada’s Guidance on Alcohol and Health. The document is a summary for the public of the new guidance. For more information, visit ccsa.ca.

- Your health care provider.
- Other parents who have breastfed or chestfed toddlers.
- Breastfeeding or chestfeeding support groups (see Resources).
- A public health nurse.
- A lactation consultant (bclca.ca).

### Healthy Eating During Breastfeeding or Chestfeeding

Lactating parents do not need a special diet. The amount of food needed may or may not change compared with pregnancy. Trust your intuition: eat when hungry. A little extra food, such as an additional snack, may help meet energy and nutrient needs. Try:

- Eating a variety of foods every day (see Canada’s food guide).
- Ensuring you get key nutrients, including vitamin A, vitamin B12, vitamin D, omega-3 and calcium (see _Baby’s Best Chance_, “Key Nutrients During Pregnancy”).
- Eating more if you are hungrier than usual.
- Drinking water as your main drink choice.
- Continuing to take a multivitamin supplement with folic acid.

Your diet does not have to be perfect; do the best you can. Supports are available for barriers to healthy food: visit healthlinkbc.ca or call 8-1-1. Many foods avoided during pregnancy, such as unpasteurized cheeses and sushi, are no longer safety concerns while breastfeeding or chestfeeding. Foods and drinks still best avoided include fish high in mercury, items high in caffeine, alcohol, and herbal teas other than those safe during pregnancy. See canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/natural-non-prescription.html.

## When Your Toddler Stops Breastfeeding or Chestfeeding

Breastfeeding or chestfeeding is recommended for up to 2 years, or longer if parent and child want. The toddler may stop naturally (“child-led”), or the parent may decide to wean (“parent-led”). Either way, weaning can be emotional. Do what works best for both.

If you choose parent-led weaning:

- Start when no other major family change is happening, such as returning to work or school, so you do not add stress to an already difficult situation.
- Offer extra comfort and cuddles so your toddler still gets attention and love.
- Go slowly. Gradual weaning over weeks, months or longer lets milk reduce slowly, helping avoid sore breasts or chest and giving both of you time to adjust emotionally, establish routines and find new ways to feel close.
- Do not offer, but do not refuse. Keep your toddler busy with other activities and avoid usual feeding places. When they ask to breastfeed or chestfeed, take time to enjoy it together.

To begin, replace one daily feed with expressed milk in an open cup. If you do not provide your own milk, use infant formula. Before 9 months, use store-bought infant formula; after 9 months, whole (3.25%) cow’s milk can be used. If older than 6 months, a feeding can also be replaced with solid food. Every 5 to 7 days, or once you and your child are comfortable, replace another feed. Save the most comforting feed (usually first thing in the morning or bedtime) for last. Finally replace that last feed. As children learn to feed themselves solid foods, they naturally begin to wean at their own pace. If it is not going smoothly or your child is sick and needs comfort, take a break. When one daily feed remains, the other parent or caregiver can take over; the child may find a cup of milk from someone other than the breastfeeding or chestfeeding parent easier.

## Supplementing for 6- to 9-Month-Olds

Some families need or choose to supplement a breastfed or chestfed 6- to 9-month-old for medical or personal reasons. **Supplementing** means giving the child your expressed milk, donated human milk or infant formula in addition to breastfeeding or chestfeeding.

Your own fresh milk, or frozen milk thawed just before use, is best. If another option is needed, the recommended order is:

1. Your own milk, freshly hand-expressed or pumped.
2. Your own frozen milk, thawed just before use.
3. Pasteurized donor milk from a certified human milk bank.
4. Store-bought cow’s-milk-based infant formula (see Formula Feeding for 6- to 9-Month-Olds).

Certified milk banks have a small supply, usually prescribed for premature, very ill or high-risk babies. Donors are carefully screened and milk is pasteurized to kill harmful bacteria and viruses. If milk from a certified bank is not available, store-bought cow’s-milk-based infant formula is the next choice and provides complete nutrition. It is available ready-to-feed liquid, concentrated liquid and powdered. For a 6- to 9-month-old with a weakened immune system, use liquid formula—ready-to-feed or concentrate. Powdered formula is not sterile and should not be used for children with weakened immune systems, who get sick more easily. Use specialized formula only on a health care provider’s recommendation.

Before supplementing, follow hunger and fullness cues and talk with your health care provider before giving a 6- to 9-month-old anything other than your own milk. Unneeded other milk or formula can decrease milk supply, cause engorgement, make breastfeeding or chestfeeding harder and affect the child’s health. Sometimes supplementation is medically necessary or chosen to provide extra food and energy. The most important things are that the child gets enough food and is fed safely, whether with human milk, formula or both. Always follow formula-label directions for making and storing formula.

If you supplement:

- Give your child as much of your own milk as you can.
- Get support from your health care provider or a lactation consultant to keep up your own milk supply. This may allow you to return fully to breastfeeding or chestfeeding in the future.
- Use a spoon or a small cup without a lid, not a bottle. Or use a specialized feeding device if recommended by your health care professional.

### Informal Milk Sharing

**Informal (“peer-to-peer”) human milk sharing** means sharing unscreened, unpasteurized human milk with friends, family or local or online milk-sharing groups. Parents who cannot produce enough milk sometimes consider it. Before giving a child milk from an informal donor, talk with your health care provider about risks and benefits.

Health Canada, the Canadian Paediatric Society and the Human Milk Banking Association of North America do not recommend sharing human milk with friends, family or milk-sharing groups. Informally shared milk is not tested or pasteurized. Risks include:

- HIV and hepatitis B and C can pass to the child; donors may not know they carry viruses or bacteria.
- If donors smoke, drink alcohol, use cannabis, take prescription or over-the-counter medications, herbal supplements, or non-prescription opioids and stimulants, harmful substances can pass into milk.
- If milk is not collected, stored and transported safely, bacteria can grow and make the child sick.
- Unscreened milk may be mixed with water, cow’s milk or something else unknown to the recipient.

If considering informal milk sharing, lower the risk by not buying milk online; using only a close family member or friend; learning about the donor’s health and lifestyle; ensuring recent negative tests for hepatitis B and C, HIV, human T-lymphotropic virus and syphilis, with no ongoing exposure risk; confirming the donor does not smoke, drink alcohol, use cannabis or non-prescription opioids and stimulants, or take medications or supplements (including herbs); ensuring the donor is in good overall health and not using milk when sick; limiting the number of donors; maintaining ongoing face-to-face contact; and ensuring milk is handled, stored and sent as safely as possible. In-home heat treatment (“flash heating”) has not been proven to remove dangerous bacteria and viruses.

If you have extra milk to donate, talk with your health care provider or midwife, visit bcwomensmilkbank.ca or call 604-875-3743 to contact the BC Women’s Provincial Milk Bank. If considering supplementing, discuss the decision with your health care provider or public health nurse, or call HealthLink BC at 8-1-1.
