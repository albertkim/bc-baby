# Formula, Cleaning Feeding Equipment, Solid Foods, and Drinking From a Cup

_Source pages: 49-53_

## Formula Feeding for 6- to 9-Month-Olds

Deciding how to feed a 6- to 9-month-old is not always easy. Breastfeeding or chestfeeding is best for a child’s growth and development. For medical or personal reasons, store-bought infant formula may be used instead of or in addition to human milk.

### Make an Informed Choice

Get the information you need to make an informed choice. Contact your health care provider or public health nurse, or call HealthLink BC at 8-1-1. They can discuss the benefits, risks and costs of each option and help choose a formula type if you decide to use formula.

The World Health Organization, Health Canada, Dietitians of Canada, the Canadian Paediatric Society and the B.C. Ministry of Health recommend only human milk for the first 6 months. After a baby is eating solid family foods, human milk remains an important source of nutrition. If possible, continue breastfeeding or chestfeeding until the child is 2 years or older.

### If You Use Formula

Breastfeed or chestfeed too, if you can. Give human milk whenever possible; if using formula, try to give it in addition to your own milk (see Supplementing for 6- to 9-Month-Olds). If you are not currently breastfeeding or chestfeeding but hope to in the future, talk with your health care provider about maintaining supply or find a lactation coach through the British Columbia Lactation Consultants Association (bclca.ca).

Follow formula-label directions to prepare it correctly. Safe preparation and storage are important to your baby’s health. Ask your health care provider or public health nurse, call HealthLink BC at 8-1-1, or see healthlinkbc.ca/sites/default/files/documents/hfile69b_0.pdf with questions. Health Canada inspects store-bought infant formula for safety and nutrition, but formula can come into contact with bacteria or be missing an ingredient. Check recalls at recalls-rappels.canada.ca/en.

Choose only store-bought cow’s-milk-based infant formula, or store-bought soy-based formula if your child cannot have cow’s milk. Other drinks do not provide the nutrition a 6- to 9-month-old needs. Never substitute evaporated or condensed milk; regular cow’s, goat’s or other animal milk (allowed only after 9 to 12 months); nut drinks such as almond, cashew or coconut; other “milks” such as rice, oat, potato, soy or hemp drinks; or homemade formula.

A home machine for preparing infant formula can be unsafe. It may not heat water enough to kill bacteria in formula and may not dispense the right amount of powder. The safest way to prepare powdered formula is with boiled water cooled to 70°C.

Although human milk is best for a 6- to 9-month-old, sometimes formula is necessary. If you need or want to use formula but feel uncomfortable or guilty, talk with your health care provider. Whatever the feeding method, feeding times can build a close, loving bond.

### Water for Preparing Formula

In most B.C. communities, drinking water is treated at a water treatment plant and is typically safe at the tap. If you have your own water source, such as a private well, test it regularly. See healthlinkbc.ca/healthlinkbc-files/well-water-testing. Some buildings have plumbing containing lead, which can leach into drinking water and affect infants and children. See healthlinkbc.ca/healthlinkbc-files/lead-drinking-water.

Be sure the water is safe before using it for infant formula. Do not use hot-side tap water; discoloured water that has not been tested; specialty nursery or baby waters; carbonated or flavoured waters; or water known to contain high levels of nitrate, fluoride, sodium, lead, manganese or blue-green algae (cyanobacteria). If you do not have access to safe water or are unsure whether it is safe, use ready-to-feed liquid formula or make powdered formula with bottled water. Boiling will not remove dangerous chemicals from water and may make them more concentrated. For questions or concerns about drinking water, contact your local health unit, health authority or First Nations government office; see gov.bc.ca/gov/content/environment/air-land-water/water/water-quality/drinking-water-quality/health-authority-contacts.

## Cleaning and Disinfecting Feeding Equipment

Keep all equipment used to feed your child clean. Clean breast or chest pumps according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Other items—including equipment used to make formula, artificial nipples, rings, caps, discs, measuring cups, can openers, storage containers and tongs—can be cleaned and disinfected in the kitchen. Dishwashers are not recommended for disinfecting feeding equipment.

### Clean and Disinfect Feeding Equipment

**First, clean everything:**

1. Wash your hands with soap and warm water.
2. Wash the sink with a clean dishcloth, soap and warm water.
3. Disinfect the counter by mixing 1 teaspoon (5 ml) of bleach with 2 cups (500 ml) of water in a labelled spray bottle. Spray the counter and wipe it with a clean towel.
4. Fill the sink with hot, soapy water.
5. Wash all infant-feeding equipment, including tongs.
6. Scrub the inside of containers and artificial nipples with a clean bottle brush.
7. Rinse everything with hot water and set it on a clean towel.

**Then, disinfect everything:**

1. Fill a large pot with water and add the clean items. Ensure everything is covered and there are no air pockets.
2. Bring the water to a boil and boil uncovered for 2 minutes.
3. Remove the tongs. Once cool, use them to remove the other items.
4. Set everything on a clean towel to air dry.
5. When dry, use the items right away or store them in a clean plastic bag or on a clean, dry towel covered with another clean towel.

If using a store-bought disinfector or sterilizer, follow the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Introducing Solid Foods

A baby may be ready for solid foods when all of these are true:

- They are about 6 months old.
- They can sit and hold their head up.
- They can turn their head away to show they are full.
- They try to pick up food and put it in their mouth.

### How to Introduce Solid Food

1. Choose a time when your toddler is wide awake and has an appetite but is not too hungry. Solids can be offered before or after breastfeeding or chestfeeding.
2. Sit them in a feeding chair facing you.
3. Eat with your toddler so they can learn by watching you.
4. Serve the same foods as the rest of the family, in an appropriate texture and without added sugar or salt.

Gagging is normal when learning to eat. When food slips to the back of the tongue before the child is ready to swallow, they gag to prevent choking (see Reducing Choking Hazards). Stay calm and reassure them; if you panic, they may become afraid to try new foods.

### First Foods and Preparation

First foods should be rich in iron, such as meat, fish, chicken, eggs, lentils, tofu and iron-fortified baby cereal. Introduce these alongside other family foods such as grains, vegetables and fruit.

Meat, fish or shellfish are easier to eat when moist and in tiny pieces. Mix small pieces with water, human milk, mashed vegetables or gravy to make an even texture; braise and stew meat to make it soft enough to chew, or shred or grind it; and use nutrient-rich dark meat rather than white chicken meat. Remove bones and shells from fish.

Cook foods to these internal temperatures:

| Food                                                   | Internal temperature and doneness          |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------ |
| Pork                                                   | 71°C                                       |
| Ground beef, lamb and veal                             | 71°C                                       |
| Game meats                                             | 74°C                                       |
| Well-done pieces and whole cuts of beef, lamb and veal | 77°C                                       |
| Eggs                                                   | 74°C, until the yolks are hard             |
| Poultry and game birds: parts and ground meat          | 74°C                                       |
| Poultry and game birds: whole birds                    | 82°C                                       |
| Fish                                                   | 70°C; fully cooked fish flakes with a fork |
| Oysters                                                | 90°C                                       |
| Other shellfish                                        | 71°C                                       |

### Keeping Homemade Baby Food Safe

Before and after preparing food, wash counters and utensils with soap and water. Disinfect surfaces and equipment with 1 teaspoon (5 ml) bleach mixed with 3 cups (750 ml) water. This is especially important after handling and preparing raw meat and fish. Throw out worn cutting boards, which can hide germs. Refrigerate leftovers and use within 2 days, or freeze and use within 2 months.

### Safely Reheating Baby Food in a Microwave

Stir at least once halfway through to ensure even heating. Taste the food to ensure it is not too hot before giving it to your child. Do not heat bottles in the microwave; warm them in hot water instead. Use only plastic containers or plastic wrap labelled “microwave safe.” Do not use damaged, stained or smelly containers. Use a microwave only to reheat cooked food, not to cook raw food.

## Drinking From a Cup

A toddler can eat the same foods the family normally eats. Soft foods may be mashed, ground, minced or pureed. Finger foods include small pieces of well-cooked vegetables, soft fruit without skins, cooked pasta or grated cheese.

Once a child can sit and hold their head up—usually around 6 months—they are likely ready to learn to drink from an open cup between breastfeedings or chestfeedings. Choose a small cup that fits their hands and mouth. Start with a little water, help them hold it and bring it to their mouth, and expect spills.

### Sip Cups and Bottles

Sip cups do not help toddlers learn to drink from a cup because the spill-preventing valve makes them suck rather than sip. If supplementing with expressed human milk or infant formula, a toddler can use a small open cup instead of a bottle. Toddlers can use a bottle until about 12 months; after 12 months bottles are not necessary. Using a bottle for anything other than water can lead to tooth decay (see Looking After Your Toddler’s Teeth).

If a child is used to drinking milk from a bottle, try slowly decreasing the amount of milk in each bottle. If they want more to drink, offer a small amount of water in a separate bottle. Use an open cup with meals and snacks. If your child uses a bottle, start switching to an open cup by about 12 months; they may not use bottles anymore by 18 months.
