## Sexual Development

The way you touch, care for and talk about your toddler’s body sends important messages. Think about your own values and beliefs and what you want to communicate about sexuality. Consider using medical or scientific terms for all body parts, from nose to vagina; remembering that body curiosity is normal; thinking ahead about how to answer questions so you feel less anxious; and talking about what is okay in public and what is meant for private spaces.

**Try this:** Let your toddler decide who they want to hug and kiss. This teaches them they have control over their body.

### Sexual Development by Age

From birth to 24 months, a child may enjoy being naked, like touching parts of their body including genitals, and get erections or vaginal lubrication as a natural reflex.

From 24 to 36 months, a child may touch their genitals or masturbate, notice gender differences, explore bodies (“play doctor or nurse”) with a same-age playmate, and be interested in body functions.

It is as normal for a child to touch their genitals as it is for them to explore their toes or fingers. Toddlers are naturally curious about their bodies and quickly learn that touching their genitals feels good.

If your toddler touches themself in public, remind them that genitals are personal areas, best explored in private spaces such as their bedroom. Offer a toy or another distraction. If they explore genitals with another child of similar age, calmly tell them their genitals are private and should not be touched or looked at by others without permission. Redirect them to another activity and ask if they have questions. Reassure your child they have not done anything wrong, while emphasizing that no one should touch or see their genitals without their consent. If you have concerns, discuss them with your health care provider.

### Signs That May Need Attention

There can be many reasons for changes in a child’s behaviour, but some may signal abuse. Talk with your health care provider if your child:

- Develops a strong, constant focus on their own and others’ genitals.
- Does not follow your rules about respecting and not touching others’ private areas.
- Has discharge, swelling or bruising in the genital area.

## Gender Identity

Children usually begin to show interest in gender early in life. At 2 to 3 years old, they see differences between male and female bodies. Most children call themselves a “boy” or a “girl.” This may or may not match the sex assigned at birth, and may change over time.

Most children identify with the sex assigned at birth. Others may realize their gender identity does not match their assigned sex. Some children may switch between calling themselves a “boy” and a “girl,” or avoid being labelled. All children may express identity in creative ways. Exploring gender identity is a normal and healthy part of development. All children do best when they know they are loved and accepted for who they are.

- **Assigned sex** is the sex assigned to a baby at birth. A newborn with a penis is assigned “male”; a newborn with a vulva is assigned “female.” In rare cases, a child is born with external sex organs that are not clearly male or female; the assigned sex is “intersex.”
- **Gender identity** is who a person feels themself to be. A child may identify as a boy or girl, neither, or may be uncertain; that is okay.
- **Gender expression** is how a person expresses themself to others, including how they act, what they wear, how they do their hair and the name they choose. It is not necessarily the same as gender identity. For example, a boy may like dressing in dresses but not identify as a girl.
- **Transgender (trans)** describes a person whose gender identity does not match the sex assigned at birth.
- **Two-Spirit** is a term used by many Indigenous people who have diverse genders and sexualities. Two-Spirit people were historically revered in most nations and may have different roles, gifts, language and appearances depending on their nation. Two-Spirit identity is closely connected to Indigenous culture and spirituality; a non-Indigenous person cannot personally use this term.

A child’s gender identity cannot be determined from their choice of toys or clothes. Gender expression does not necessarily signal who they know themselves to be.

Support healthy gender development by loving your child and being proud of them for who they are, without pressuring them to change; talking with them about gender and showing examples of different gender expressions; allowing them to wear clothes and play with toys they like; showing them that people of all genders exist and live happy lives; and watching for bullying.

### Finding Support

Many parents and caregivers find it hard to accept that a child’s gender identity may differ from their assigned sex. A child worried about gender may be depressed, anxious or have trouble concentrating. Transgender children may be especially uncomfortable with their assigned sex at the start of puberty, when their bodies begin to change.

For support parenting a transgender or gender-creative child, contact:

- Trans Care BC: 1-866-999-1514 or phsa.ca/transcarebc
- Pflag Canada: pflagcanada.ca

Support is also available through mental health professionals who specialize in care of transgender and gender-creative children; your family doctor or pediatrician; Indigenous 2SLGBTQIA+ and Two-Spirit Elders or leaders; and other parents and caregivers of transgender and gender-creative children.

There is nothing physically or psychologically wrong with a child whose gender identity does not match the sex assigned at birth.

## Independence

**Independence** is a child’s growing ability to do things for themself. Between 12 and 36 months, toddlers learn that they can control themself, their body and, sometimes, people around them. This is a healthy and important developmental step. They will want to learn new skills, make choices and do things by themselves, and show more willpower to do things “their way” rather than yours.

It is okay for your toddler to feel frustrated. Letting them feel frustrated while working on simple tasks is part of learning to solve problems.

Help your toddler become independent by:

- Letting them do what they can on their own, from dressing to washing.
- Letting them make simple choices, such as which cup to drink from.
- Encouraging them to do things by themself, even if they need extra time.
- Encouraging new activities they are likely to do well.
- Giving them time to repeat a skill as they learn; for example, let them master stacking 2 blocks before giving them a third.
- Being positive about their efforts, whether or not they succeed.

## Empathy

**Empathy** is the ability to understand and care about how others feel. It is a complex part of development that can be hard for a child to grasp, but learning to feel and show empathy is important to growing into a responsible and caring person. Your toddler can start learning empathy by watching you.

- Respond to your child’s needs and the needs of others in a caring and fair way.
- Help them name feelings, understand why they are happening and talk about what to do about them.
- Help them understand how their actions can affect people positively or negatively.
- Use positive discipline to help them grow and develop (see Positive Discipline).
- Show them how to share and be kind.
- Show them how to apologize when appropriate.

**Brain Builder:** Point out when your child has done something kind (“Look how happy you made Jessica by sharing your toy”) or not so kind (“When you hit your sibling, it made her sad”). Helping them understand how their actions affect others builds social and emotional skills.
