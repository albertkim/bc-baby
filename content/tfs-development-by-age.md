# Typical Child Development by Age: Physical, Social, Emotional, Cognitive, and Language

_Source pages: 20-29_

_Handbook: Toddler’s First Steps, 4th edition, updated in 2024._

Most toddlers develop at about the same pace. Sometimes a child progresses more quickly or slowly than expected, or has periods when they seem to take a step back. If you are concerned, see If You Have Concerns About Your Toddler’s Development and talk with your health care provider.

## Physical Development

### 6 to 9 months

- Sits steadily without help.
- Stands firmly when held.
- Bounces and shuffles around on their bottom.
- Rakes at tiny objects with their hands, picks up larger objects with their fingers, and grabs, shakes and bangs things together.
- **Try this:** Make lots of time for crawling and tummy time. Give them something to hold and shake. Get down on the floor and roll a ball to them.

### 9 to 12 months

- Chews finely minced foods with teeth or gums.
- Stands by pushing off from a squat.
- Walks while holding onto furniture or your hands.
- Crawls well, including up stairs.
- Feeds themself small pieces of food.
- Uses the tip of their index finger and thumb to pick up small things.
- Puts one block on top of another.
- **Try this:** Hold their hand or stand behind them as they climb stairs. Show them how to stack blocks. Let them use activity tables or other toys where they can press buttons and make noises. Offer foods with different textures.

### 12 to 18 months

- Walks on their own.
- Crawls or walks up stairs, putting both feet on one step while holding a railing or your hand.
- Climbs on things (such as chairs) and out of things (such as strollers).
- Drinks from a cup.
- Turns the pages of a book.
- Stacks 3 or more blocks.
- Scribbles with a big crayon.
- **Try this:** Give them balls to play with. Play music and dance together. Let them stack and nest plastic bowls and match lids to pots. Offer big crayons and paper, and help them solve simple, large-piece puzzles.

### 18 to 24 months

- Kicks a ball.
- Squats while playing.
- Backs into a chair to sit down.
- Rides a small-wheeled toy.
- Takes off their own shoes, hat and socks.
- Lifts a cup and drinks, then puts it down.
- **Try this:** Offer shape-sorting activities and finger paints. Play with plastic containers in the bath. Run and kick balls together. Let them try a ride-on toy, and encourage them to dress and undress on their own.

### 24 to 30 months

- Walks backwards and sideways.
- Walks up and down stairs alone, putting both feet on one step.
- Jumps in place, lifting both feet off the floor.
- Moves on a riding toy using both feet at the same time.
- Holds a crayon in their whole hand and scribbles.
- **Try this:** Play movement games where you stop and go, change directions, and move fast and slow. Let them dress themselves, helping with buttons and zippers only when needed. Give them markers, crayons and puzzles. Let them help with simple kitchen tasks.

### 30 to 36 months

- Walks a few steps on a narrow beam.
- Climbs a slide’s ladder.
- Pedals a tricycle.
- Copies circles, dots, lines and swirls.
- Cuts paper with small safety scissors.
- Turns handles and doorknobs.
- **Try this:** Play tag and follow the leader. Roll down hills together and pretend you are animals. Let them turn pages while you read. Offer dress-up clothes with snaps, buttons and zippers.
- **Did you know?** Between ages 2 and 3, some toddlers learn to walk up and down stairs, one foot after the other, while holding the handrail.

**Family story:** “When our daughter was just starting to move around, I would lay on the floor with a toy just out of her reach. She would work hard to get to me. It kept her busy and active, and was a nice way for me to relax at the end of the day.”

Encourage your toddler’s physical development at every stage by:

- Being active together.
- Spending lots of time playing outdoors and at playgrounds.
- Helping them practise walking.
- Cheering them on when they try something new.
- Limiting screen time (see Screen Time).

**Brain builder**

Tell them what they’re doing as they’re doing it (“You’re climbing the stairs!” “You’re drawing with a crayon!”).

## Social Development

### 6 to 9 months

- Plays social games such as peekaboo and patty cake.
- Wants to do things with others.
- Points to things for a reason.
- Tries to get attention.
- Watches others.
- Notices when people enter a room.
- **Try this:** Play seeking games (“Where’s Michael?”) to teach them they are not part of you. Invite others to join in or take part in a playgroup. Let them approach new people at their own pace.

### 9 to 12 months

- Knows when you like or dislike their behaviour.
- Holds out arms and legs while being dressed.
- Copies someone when asked.
- Notices children and what they are doing.
- Repeats sounds or movements that make you laugh.
- Takes turns with you in simple games.
- Notices what others are looking at.
- Follows where you point, then looks back to you.
- **Try this:** Talk about what activities will happen next in the day.

### 12 to 18 months

- Likes to be the centre of attention.
- Starts to show a sense of humour.
- Plays best by themself and does not share toys.
- Copies adult activities such as reading and talking on the phone.
- Separates from you for brief periods.
- Calls for you.
- **Try this:** Make up a goodbye routine, such as a big kiss, wave and special words. Offer choices, such as whether to put on their coat or shoes first, and let them help with simple chores.

### 18 to 24 months

- Likes playing alone for short periods.
- Is better at playing beside other children than playing with them.
- Says “no” and “mine” often and has trouble sharing.
- Likes doing things without help.
- **Try this:** Let them know what to expect in new situations. Talk about family and friends. Point out what other children are doing and introduce your child to a playmate.

### 24 to 30 months

- Has trouble sharing.
- Likes to do more for themself.
- Likes being near other children in parallel play.
- Is learning to be comfortable around new people.
- **Try this:** Give them chances to play one-on-one with another child and do some things without help. Use their toys to model sharing (“Look! Dolly’s sharing their blocks with Teddy.”). Spend quiet time reading, telling stories and cuddling. Praise positive behaviour.
- **Parallel play** means playing beside or near other children. **Cooperative play** means playing with other children. Most children prefer parallel play until they are 30 to 36 months old.
- **Did you know?** It is common for toddlers to be afraid of new people and act shy around strangers.

### 30 to 36 months

- Hugs, kisses and shows affection.
- Uses social language such as “thank you” and “bye-bye”.
- Plays with others and takes turns more easily.
- Plays make-believe games and creates imaginary characters.
- **Try this:** Give your toddler lots of affection and encouragement. Join imaginary play. Introduce them to neighbours and let them play with other children, staying nearby to supervise and help solve problems.
- **Did you know?** Around age 3, some toddlers create an imaginary friend.

Encourage your toddler’s social development at every stage by:

- Responding when they want help or attention.
- Keeping to routines.
- Eating together at the family table.
- Using “please” and “thank you” to model good manners.
- Letting them be around people and play with other children.
- Playing games with them.

## Emotional Development

### 6 to 9 months

- Shows strong likes and dislikes.
- Laughs.
- Wants to stay with you or other trusted adults.
- Shows when scared.
- **Try this:** Smile at them, make lots of eye contact and practise other serve-and-return interactions (see Brain Development).
- **Did you know?** Many infants and toddlers are affected and even scared by loud noises such as vacuum cleaners.
- **Family story:** “Whenever one of us left for the day, we had a special routine. We would all hug and say, ‘Let’s kiss Daddy,’ ‘Let’s kiss Mommy,’ and then, ‘Let’s kiss Taylor.’ It was fun, took just a minute and made leaving easier for everyone.”

### 9 to 12 months

- Shows many emotions.
- Is upset when they do something wrong.
- Wants comfort when upset.
- Needs to be within sight and hearing of a parent or caregiver.
- Shows affection with hugs, kisses, pats and smiles.
- **Try this:** Offer simple choices, such as which cup to use, and ask them for lots of hugs and kisses.
- **Brain Builder:** Starting when your child is about 9 months old, focus on helping them understand and deal with emotions. Talk about how they feel (“I see you’re feeling sad”), how you feel (“I’m frustrated”) and how you handle emotions in a healthy way (“I’m going to take some deep breaths”).

### 12 to 18 months

- Likes familiar places.
- Takes risks if a trusted adult is present.
- Recognizes themself in mirrors and photos.
- Hugs and kisses you and other very familiar people.
- Likes to be the centre of attention.
- **Try this:** Let them play on their own. Talk about upcoming changes to their routine, and find ways to help them feel successful.
- **Use praise to build self-esteem:** Save praise for things that take effort rather than praising every small act. Do not base praise on appearance. Be specific (“You did a great job cleaning up your toys,” rather than “good job”). Offer encouragement and empathy when they struggle with something new: “I can see you’re trying hard to zip up your jacket. It can be tricky, but if you keep trying I know you’ll get it. If you need a break, though, I can help this time.”

### 18 to 24 months

- Shows concern for others.
- Shows fear but can be settled.
- Sometimes wants to do things alone and sometimes wants help.
- Is watchful around new adults.
- **Try this:** Talk about how others feel and how the toddler’s actions affect them (“I think John feels sad because you took away his toy”). Offer choices to help them cope with their own feelings (“You’re feeling sad. Do you want to cuddle or be alone?”).
- **Did you know?** Many toddlers like a security blanket or toy. A toddler may suddenly be frightened by situations that did not bother them before.

### 24 to 30 months

- Wants to get their own way.
- Likes routines.
- Acts out emotions through play (for example, roaring like an angry lion).
- Has strong feelings but trouble expressing them.
- **Try this:** Encourage them to show and talk about emotions. Make transitions easier by saying what to expect (“In 5 minutes it will be time to pick up your sibling from school”).

### 30 to 36 months

- Gets upset by big changes to routines.
- Responds to other people’s feelings.
- Wants to do things themself but may fear new things.
- Wants approval and needs praise.
- **Try this:** Sing songs and read stories about emotions. Talk about how characters are feeling and why.

Encourage your toddler’s emotional development at every stage by:

- Creating lots of structure and routines.
- Holding, cuddling and comforting them—especially when they are upset, sick, scared or hurt.
- Practising serve-and-return interactions (see Brain Development).
- Using positive discipline (see Positive Discipline).
- Offering them choices.
- Talking about feelings—yours, theirs and other people’s.

## Cognitive Development

### 6 to 9 months

- Notices the size of objects and knows if things are near or far.
- Briefly searches for an object that has been taken away.
- Understands how things can be used, such as shaking a noisemaker or pushing a button.
- **Try this:** Play copying games such as clapping or sticking your tongue out at each other. Hide things briefly, then reveal them. Play in-and-out games such as putting blocks in a container and taking them out again.

### 9 to 12 months

- Connects animals with the sounds they make.
- Has a better memory.
- Sees you as separate from themself and points when asked “Who’s Mommy?”.
- Recognizes their own name.
- Starts to understand cause and effect, such as things falling when dropped.
- Matches shapes, such as putting a cube in a square hole.
- **Try this:** Take turns doing things, such as blowing kisses. Ask them to help find lost objects. Talk about cause and effect (“You dropped Teddy, so now he’s on the floor”).
- **Family story:** “Whenever my friend came over, she would pull things out from her pockets to show our 10-month-old and say the names of the things carefully. My toddler would often try and copy what my friend said. It was a good example of what we needed to be doing every day.”

### 12 to 18 months

- Realizes things still exist even when they cannot see them.
- Finds things in pictures.
- Learns by touching and moving things, such as fitting things into holes.
- Expects events to follow routines.
- Follows simple directions such as “show me the ball.”.
- **Try this:** Count things together. Talk about events and people they remember. Ask them to point to pictures in books and give simple directions (“Put your truck and doll in the toy box, please”).
- **Brain Builder:** It is healthy for a toddler to feel a little frustration while trying to do something. By not rushing in to help, you teach problem-solving skills.

### 18 to 24 months

- Uses things as intended, such as putting a phone to their ear.
- Understands the passing of time and words such as “not now”.
- Names familiar people in photos.
- Has a better memory.
- **Try this:** Give them crayons, markers and paper, and 2- to 4-piece puzzles. Talk about time (“We’re going to Grandma’s tomorrow”) and sounds they hear, such as car horns or barking dogs. Challenge their skills—for example, offer a fourth block once they can stack 3.

### 24 to 30 months

- Pretend-plays with others.
- Matches and sorts some shapes and colours.
- Starts to understand the order of numbers.
- Has a longer attention span.
- Starts solving problems by trial and error.
- **Try this:** Let them fill in blanks when singing or reading together. Play matching and sorting games.

### 30 to 36 months

- Compares sizes and uses words such as “bigger” and “smaller”.
- Play-acts, such as pretending to be a dinosaur.
- Counts 3 things.
- Matches and sorts things.
- Enjoys creative movement.
- **Try this:** Talk about number order (“Who’s first?”), encourage storytelling and ask what they think about things. Let them try 3- to 6-piece puzzles. Give them clothes and props for dress-up.

Encourage your toddler’s cognitive development at every stage by:

- Offering them a variety of toys, puzzles and art supplies.
- Reading with them and talking about the pictures (see Reading to Your Toddler).
- Dancing and singing together.
- Counting together.
- Talking about the shapes and colours of things.
- Playing games with rules, like Simon Says.
- Giving them plenty of praise as they learn new skills.

See the Brain Builder boxes throughout this guide for ideas on boosting your toddler’s cognitive development.

## Language Development

### 6 to 9 months

- Says several sounds (“ma mu da di ba”) in one breath.
- Babbles and repeats sounds (“da da da”).
- Responds to some words, such as “Mommy” or “ball”.
- Turns to listen to familiar sounds, such as a ringing phone.
- Looks when you say their name.
- Shakes their head to say “no.”.
- **Try this:** Have a “conversation” by responding to babbling with your own sounds or words. Use simple sentences about what you are doing (“We’re looking at the dog”). It is never too soon to share books: they may flip pages, talk about what they see or look at words and pictures (see Reading to Your Toddler).

### 9 to 12 months

- Babbles in longer strings of sounds such as “bababa” or “badagee”.
- Responds to simple instructions with familiar words.
- Takes turns making sounds with you.
- Copies speech sounds.
- **Try this:** Read to them every day (see Reading to Your Toddler). If you speak more than one language, use both from the start. One parent or caregiver can use one language while another uses another. Read to your child in both languages, too.
- **Did you know?** Many toddlers leave out words such as “the” and “in” and endings such as “-ing” and “-s” while learning to speak English.

### 12 to 18 months

- Says 5 or more words.
- Understands many more words than they can say and points when asked where their belly button is.
- Uses “no” correctly, often with a head shake.
- Tries to sing songs.
- Makes animal sounds and other sound effects.
- Uses gestures such as clapping, blowing a kiss or giving a “high 5”.
- Puts a finger to their lips to say “shhh.”.
- **Try this:** Read using different voices and expression. Sing. Give them books to look at and encourage them to point out things in pictures. Keep a diary of words they say.
- **Did you know?** You do not need to teach a toddler to read and write. Reading, talking and singing build language skills that form the basis of later literacy.

### 18 to 24 months

- Uses 2- or 3-word sentences.
- Understands about 200 words.
- Says (clearly or not) about 50 words.
- Talks to themself or stuffed animals.
- Names pictures in books.
- Copies new words and phrases.
- Asks “what” and “where” questions.
- **Try this:** Listen carefully and ask simple questions about what they are saying. Take them to the library.
- **Family story:** “I talked to my daughter about anything and everything we were doing. When crossing the street, for example, I’d say, ‘Is it safe to cross? Let’s look for cars. There are no cars. It’s safe. Let’s go.’ All that talking really helped her learn words, and I wasn’t surprised when she spoke at an early age.”

### 24 to 30 months

- Uses personal pronouns such as “I,” “me” and “you”.
- Says their name based on what they hear themself being called.
- Answers simple questions such as “What’s your name?”.
- Likes looking at books and talking about pictures.
- Sings parts of songs.
- Asks lots of questions.
- **Try this:** Repeat what they say to show interest. Look at family pictures together and describe them in simple sentences.

### 30 to 36 months

- Can often be understood.
- Asks lots of questions.
- Tells stories and sings.
- May repeat 5-word sentences.
- Uses social words such as “please,” “thank you” and “hello”.
- Names most body parts.
- **Try this:** Ask about things that interest them, using open-ended questions such as “What did you see on your walk?” or “What do you think?” Sing number songs and make up silly rhymes.

Encourage your toddler’s language development at every stage by:

- Talking to them about everything from what you are doing (“Let’s wash these cups”) to what they see (“What a fluffy cat!”).
- Responding to their babbling with your own words, as though you are having a conversation.
- Pointing out the names of things around you.
- Reading to them every day.
- Building on what they say. If they say “ball,” you can say, “Yes, that’s a red ball.”
- Using nursery rhymes to help them learn language patterns.
- Singing while doing daily tasks, like changing their diaper.
