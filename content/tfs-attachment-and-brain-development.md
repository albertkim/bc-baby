# Attachment, Temperament, and Brain Development

_Source pages: 13-19_

_Handbook: Toddler’s First Steps, 4th edition, updated in 2024._

## Attachment

**Attachment** is the close relationship that gives a child a safe place from which to explore the world. A healthy attachment is one of the key factors in raising a happy and confident child. When children feel securely attached, they can try new things knowing they can return to their safe place with their caregiver. Attachment grows through everyday actions and routines. When caregivers respond lovingly and acceptingly over time, children learn they can rely on them for support and protection.

**Key takeaway:** A healthy attachment is key to raising a child who is happy and confident. Attachment grows over time through consistent, responsive and loving interactions between you and your toddler.

Building a strong attachment means:

- Comforting a child whenever they are sick, hurt, scared or upset by reassuring, rocking or holding them.
- Paying attention and responding. Spend one-on-one time doing things the child enjoys, and put aside screens and other distractions. “Serve and return” interactions are described under Brain Development.
- Following daily routines so the child knows what to expect. Keep mealtimes and bedtime predictable, and make rules clear and consistent.
- Showing trust by letting the child explore while supervised. For example, let them move a short distance as they learn to crawl or walk, while staying nearby so they can return.
- Talking with the child about events that may upset them. A new sibling’s birth or a friend moving away can cause nightmares and other distress. Even very negative experiences can be made easier by a positive, caring adult’s presence.
- Preparing the child for time apart. Leave them only with someone trusted. Set up a goodbye ritual, such as a kiss, special words and a wave from the driveway, then leave confidently. Provide activities and a photo, favourite blanket or toy for comfort. Tell them when you will return and do your best to be back on time.
- Building good memories by telling family stories, looking at photo albums together, saving crafts and artwork, writing a diary of achievements and making videos of special events.
- Creating a sense of belonging by honouring family and cultural traditions.

**Try this:** Find activities that you and your toddler both enjoy. Spend time talking, listening to them and taking them places. Read stories, go for outings and play games together.

## Temperament

**Temperament** is a child’s unique character that makes them “who they are.” It may be obvious from birth or appear over time. Temperament includes a child’s general mood, activity level, reactions to new situations, and whether they are easily distracted or can focus well. A toddler’s temperament may be similar to or very different from their caregiver’s. Home environment and people around the child have some effect, but temperament is mostly a natural part of who the child is.

Accepting a child’s temperament does not mean letting them do whatever they want. It means helping them develop in a way that is comfortable for them. **Family story:** “A nurse suggested I get down on the floor with my son and watch what he was doing while trying to imagine what he was thinking. I learned a lot about him from this simple act of quiet watching.”

Support your child’s temperament by:

- **Understanding them.** Watch them in different settings to learn their likes and dislikes and how they feel most comfortable relating to others.
- **Not labelling them.** A child who repeatedly hears that they are shy or talk too much may believe it and live up to those expectations.
- **Being open and accepting.** Let the child lead in discovering their talents and preferences instead of expecting them to be a certain way.
- **Directing their temperament into positive activities that feel comfortable.** If they are shy in a playgroup, let them sit with you and watch until ready to join. If they have lots of energy, offer movement such as dancing, running in the yard or helping with chores. If they must sit still, give them something to hold and play with.
- **Responding to what their behaviour communicates.** What looks like “naughty” behaviour may be the toddler’s way of telling you they need something. Instead of scolding, help them understand what is happening and show empathy. For example: “It seems like you’re really upset. I’m sorry you feel that way. I get upset sometimes, too.” As your child grows, help them name feelings and handle big emotions in healthy ways. Ask what they are feeling and whether there is a way to express anger without throwing toys.
- **Helping them face fears.** If frightened, tell them you have confidence they can overcome the fear and work through it together. Check the closet for monsters or hold them on your lap around dogs, for example.
- **Reducing sensory distractions.** If they are sensitive to noise, light or things touching their skin, try noise-cancelling headphones, dimmer lights or cutting tags out of clothes. This can help them focus on other things.
- **Breaking unfamiliar situations into simple steps.** If they dislike new situations, explain a process step by step—for example, “This is how you sit on your bike. This is where your feet go”—instead of saying “Jump on and let’s ride.” Give plenty of warning before routine changes.

Learning to accept and work with your child’s temperament rather than trying to change it will help them feel good about themself. And it will make both your lives happier and easier.

## Brain Development

**Key takeaway:** The experiences your child has as a toddler will affect their health, well-being and coping skills for the rest of their life.

When we think about how to support a toddler’s development, we usually consider the basics: keeping them comfortable and safe and making sure they sleep well and get enough to eat. But what about building your toddler’s brain?

During the toddler years, your child’s brain is making millions of new connections every day as they learn about the world around them. The way you respond to them can help teach their brain to build and strengthen the connections that will help them feel safe, trust others, be confident and live a healthy life. Early experiences play a big role in shaping your toddler’s brain. And while every child develops at their own pace, a healthy brain supports development in all areas.

### Serve and Return

“Serve and return” interactions mean showing attention and responding with warmth, support and enthusiasm to what a child communicates. Like a tennis game, parent and child send and receive different kinds of communication, such as touch, sounds, words, smiles and eye contact.

These interactions help build a solid foundation for the toddler’s brain and support future development. They also build attachment. Each moment may feel short and unimportant, but many such interactions in the early years build new paths in the brain and encourage learning. Each positive interaction is another brick in a healthy foundation for later development, supporting learning to read, dealing with stress and forming relationships.

To use serve and return:

- Pay lots of attention to your toddler.
- Notice what interests them, whether it is a bright colour, fluffy dog or spinning wheel.
- Talk regularly about what you are doing, what they see and what gets their attention.
- Cuddle and comfort them when they cry or are upset.
- Sing songs, play simple games and read to them.

Put your cell phone aside while playing so you can focus on your toddler. Brain Builder boxes throughout the book offer more everyday ideas. See Dealing With Common Challenging Behaviours for ways to respond in a way that supports development.

### Stress and the Developing Brain

Stress also shapes your toddler’s brain development—in both positive and negative ways. Everyone feels stress. But while some stress can help your child develop, other stress can harm them.

- **Good stress** is healthy for a child’s development. Experiences such as meeting new people can prepare children to cope with future challenges.
- **Tolerable stress** is stress a child can cope with when supported by a loving adult. Difficult experiences may be painful, but with love and adult care they do not have to harm development. With support, a child’s ability to cope with tolerable stress grows over time.
- **Toxic stress** harms development of a child’s brain and body and can cause lifelong challenges. It is the effect of serious, ongoing hardship, abuse or neglect on a child who does not have adult support.

When a child experiences toxic stress, the basic structures of their developing brain may be damaged. Without a sturdy foundation to support future development, they may be at risk for long-term health problems, developmental issues and even addiction. It is possible to fix some of the damage of childhood toxic stress later in life. But it is easier and more effective to build solid brain structure in the early years.

**Key takeaway:** Like adults, all children feel stress. As parents and caregivers, we can help our children deal with stress in a way that supports their healthy development.

If you are living through very difficult circumstances, you can turn toxic stress into tolerable stress and lessen the impact on your toddler by being a safe and loving person for them to turn to. At the same time, find the support you need to help you cope with the challenges you are facing (see Parenting Issues and Family Violence/Abuse). If you or your partner(s) experienced toxic stress when you were a child, you may benefit from extra support as you adjust to being a parent or caregiver. Try talking with your health care provider and asking about programs that may help.

## The Five Areas of Child Development

During the toddler years, your child will grow in 5 important areas:

1. **Physical:** how they use their body.
2. **Social:** how they interact with others.
3. **Emotional:** how they recognize, express and manage their feelings.
4. **Cognitive:** how they think and reason.
5. **Language:** how they communicate.

How your child develops depends on many things, including their natural genetic makeup, the support they receive and the things they experience.

**Brain Builder:** You can help your child develop in all 5 areas with “serve and return” interactions. Pay attention to them and show an interest in what they are interested in. By “returning” what they “serve,” you will encourage their curiosity and spark their enthusiasm for practising things like speech and social skills (see Brain Development).

By continuing to breastfeed or chestfeed, you will support your child’s development in all 5 areas.

### Physical Development

Your toddler will improve their gross motor skills (controlling the large muscles they need to sit, crawl, walk and run) and their fine motor skills (controlling their hands and fingers and getting the hand-eye coordination to reach for things and pick them up).

Support your toddler’s physical development by:

- Creating safe, interesting activities and places for them to explore and be active in.
- Using screen time in a healthy way (see Screen Time).
- Hugging and cuddling them.
- Providing healthy foods (see Eating Well).

When you cuddle your child, their brain releases hormones that they need to grow.

### Social Development

Your toddler will learn to make friends and get along with others. Support your toddler’s social development by:

- Helping them learn to cooperate, trust and be confident with others.
- Responding to their behaviour in warm and consistent ways (see Positive Discipline).
- Giving them ideas on how to interact with other toddlers.
- Pointing out what other children are doing or what others may be thinking or feeling.

### Emotional Development

Your toddler will learn to recognize, show and manage their feelings. Support your toddler’s emotional development by:

- Helping them learn to recognize and name their emotions.
- Helping them learn to show their feelings in a healthy way.
- Letting them know it is okay to have feelings—even big ones.
- Comforting them and staying present until big feelings have passed.

### Cognitive Development

Your toddler will learn to think, remember, imagine, gather and organize information, solve problems and use judgment. Support your toddler’s cognitive development by:

- Setting up safe and interesting play opportunities.
- Playing with them.
- Praising them.
- Talking to them.
- Speaking aloud when you are solving a problem.

### Language Development

Your toddler will learn to listen and talk—and can learn multiple languages if you speak more than one at home. Support your toddler’s language development by:

- Talking to them.
- Listening to them.
- Reading to them (see Reading to Your Toddler).
- Telling them stories.
- Singing with them.

**Brain Builder:** Talk to your child all the time. By asking them questions, chatting about what you are doing, and sharing rhymes, songs and stories, you will stimulate their brain and help them learn to speak and read.
