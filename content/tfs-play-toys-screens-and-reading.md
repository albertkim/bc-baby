# Play, Toys, Screen Time, Reading, and Developmental Concerns

_Source pages: 35-40_

_Handbook: Toddler’s First Steps, 4th edition, updated in 2024._

## Play

Play is a key way toddlers develop and learn about the world. It supports all five major areas of development:

- **Physical:** crawling, climbing, walking, running, using fingers, seeing and hearing.
- **Social:** cooperating, sharing, being polite, taking turns, and leading and following others.
- **Emotional:** identifying and dealing with feelings.
- **Cognitive:** problem-solving, imagination, figuring out how things work, and learning shapes, colours and numbers.
- **Language:** learning names for things, forming sentences, telling stories and listening.

Play helps children learn about themselves, others and the world. Giving toddlers lots of time to play supports development and gives caregivers a chance to show interest in what the child likes to do.

### Play by Age

#### 6 to 12 months: Explorer

A baby may grab at things, search for hidden things, and enjoy dumping, stacking and pouring. Try balls, sturdy toys on wheels, blocks, nesting toys, rattles and bowls of different sizes.

**Try this:** Use supervised bath time to interact with your toddler. Playing with water is fun and soothing.

#### 12 to 24 months: Mover and Shaker

A toddler may handle smaller toys, show their personality through play (active or quiet, focused or multitasking), and take part in “parallel play,” playing beside another child but not with them. Try balls to chase (and, when a little older, kick and throw), pop-up toys and sit-and-ride toys.

#### 24 to 36 months: Dreamer and Connector

A toddler may play with another child and enjoy imaginary play. Try dress-up clothes and props.

Outdoor play helps children develop in all areas. They use imagination, learn to play with others and build confidence by trying new things, such as going down a big slide for the first time. See appetitetoplay.com and outsideplay.ca for more information.

### Unstructured Outdoor Play and Playing Together

It is important for children to try things without knowing the outcome. Unstructured outdoor play can help children plan, manage risks, develop social skills, manage stress, build self-esteem and build resilience. Examples include playing on a log or climbing a tree.

Five ideas for playing together:

1. Pretend to be animals.
2. Show them how to do something you enjoy, such as puzzles or dancing.
3. Go outside to crawl over logs, look at bugs or pick stones.
4. Wash dishes or tear lettuce together.
5. Get out paper, paint and glue and be creative.

**Brain Builder:** Expand imaginary play by showing interest, asking questions and playing along.

Many toddlers are not ready to play with other children until they are 3 years or older. Encourage play by providing a variety of age-suited toys; offering choices and letting the child decide; setting aside time to play together each day; encouraging active and quiet activities; praising them and proudly showing their work; taking a break when they seem tired, hungry, uninterested or overstimulated; and providing lots of free, unscheduled playtime for creativity and imagination.

When time is short, sing or play word or guessing games while doing other things. If your toddler is in child care, observe them there for ideas for play at home. Let your child play with things already around the house, such as cardboard boxes or pots and pans. Many libraries have free kits to borrow.

## Toys

The best toys encourage toddlers to learn, explore and use imagination. They need not be fancy, expensive or specific to any gender.

- **Household items:** plastic bowls for filling and dumping; pillows for climbing and making caves; clothes for dress-up; the bath for supervised water play.
- **Classic toys:** blocks, dump trucks, stuffed animals, play food, garages and farms, books.
- **Nature’s toys:** dirt and sand, water, stones.

When putting out one toy, put another away. A toddler may be overwhelmed by too many choices, and rotating toys can be as exciting as getting something new.

### Safer Toys

Reduce overall injuries by ensuring toys are well-made and suited to your child’s age; not using toys with broken, sharp or loose pieces; ensuring a toy-box lid cannot trap your toddler or slam down on them; ensuring paint is non-toxic and not peeling; reading toy-label safety information; avoiding toys with small batteries and ensuring your toddler cannot remove batteries.

Reduce choking hazards by avoiding toys that fit in your toddler’s mouth; making sure toys are at least 3½ cm (1½ inches) wide and 6 cm (2½ inches) long; not letting your child play with things that can break into pieces, such as styrofoam and egg cartons; and not letting them suck or chew balloons. A toy that fits through a toilet paper roll is too small. Almost half of toy-related deaths are caused by choking.

Reduce strangling hazards by not using toys with strings, cords or ribbons longer than 15 cm (6 inches), and being careful with pull toys and skipping ropes. Reduce suffocation hazards by ensuring toys a toddler can climb into have a way out and air holes, and removing all packaging from new toys.

If you have to shout to be heard above a toy, it is too noisy and can damage your child’s hearing.

**Family story:** “We worried about how to keep our toddler away from her 7-year-old brother’s small toys. He decided to play with them in his own room. We put a gate on the door so his sister wouldn’t feel shut out.”

## Screen Time

**Screen time** is time spent in front of a computer, smartphone, tablet, television or gaming device. The Canadian Paediatric Society recommends no screen time for children under age 2 and a maximum of 1 hour a day for older toddlers. Overall, less screen time is better.

Excessive screen time may be associated with increased behavioural challenges, difficulty sleeping, school-readiness issues and unhealthy weight gain. Screens can take quality time away from caregiver and toddler, reduce active play and social time that support development and bonding, and overstimulate toddlers with colour, movement and sound. Although some content is better than others, most screen time teaches toddlers little or nothing. Screens display ads for unhealthy foods, violence and stereotypes.

You may not be able to eliminate screens completely; sometimes a phone or TV seems like the only way to get a few minutes to start dinner or gather your thoughts. Make screen time as healthy as possible:

- Think about when and why screens are used; do not use them out of habit, for entertainment or as background noise.
- Choose children’s TV shows with familiar characters, songs and repetition that teach and encourage participation.
- Stay nearby when your child uses screens or, better, watch together and talk about what you see.
- Make screen time positive. For example, a video call with relatives who live far away can benefit your child.

Be aware of your own screen use. When you pay attention and interact with your toddler, you help build a healthy brain and body. Try turning off notifications or putting your phone away while spending time together. Instead of giving your toddler a phone or tablet, engage them with board books, simple puzzles or toys for sorting shapes and stacking.

Make a family screen-time plan everyone agrees to. Include daily limits for children and parents or caregivers, no screens during mealtimes, no screens in children’s bedrooms and no TV as background noise. Ensure child-care providers know the rules.

## Reading to Your Toddler

Reading helps toddlers learn new words, builds imagination and develops listening, math and reading skills. Try reading each day from birth, making reading part of bedtime, letting your child turn pages if they can, keeping a book in your bag for the bus or grocery-store line, visiting the library for story time and books to bring home, leaving board books around to flip through, and rereading favourite books often.

You do not need to read every word. If your child has a favourite page or picture, let them linger on it or turn directly to it.

**Brain Builder:** Run your finger along the words; choose books with rhymes and repetition; use different voices for characters; point to objects in picture books and name them or ask your toddler to name them (ask what sounds animals make); and connect the book to your child’s world—for example, talk about a neighbour’s dog when there is a dog in the story.

## If You Have Concerns About Your Toddler’s Development

All children develop at their own pace, but most reach milestones by a set time. If you have concerns, help is available. When a toddler is about to make a developmental leap, they may seem to go backwards in another area. A child learning to walk, for example, may be less talkative for a while; this is a normal part of development. If a toddler was born early (preterm) or had serious health problems at birth, they may develop at a slower pace. A health care provider can help you support their development.

If you have concerns:

1. Talk with your health care provider or call HealthLink BC at 8-1-1.
2. Find extra support through your public health nurse, parent-and-child groups or a child-care program.
3. Ensure your concerns are followed up. Follow-up might include watching your toddler play, completing a child-development screening questionnaire, or a referral to another child-development or health professional or to a B.C. government support program such as BC Centre for Ability: bc-cfa.org/programs/supported-child-development/.
